1. Parkinson's Disease

Study-guide highlights: Cardinal signs; Swallowing safety; Nursing priorities

  • Cardinal signs (TRAP): resting 'pill-rolling' tremor, rigidity (cogwheel), akinesia/bradykinesia, postural instability. Shuffling gait and masked face.
  • Swallowing: sit upright at 90°, small bites, thickened liquids as ordered, keep suction at the bedside, SLP consult, and give levodopa on time.
  • Priorities: falls and aspiration. Watch carbidopa-levodopa for orthostatic hypotension and dyskinesia, and avoid high-protein meals at dose time.

2. Myasthenia Gravis

Study-guide highlights: Clinical manifestations; Pyridostigmine patient instructions

  • Autoimmune antibodies against ACh receptors at the NMJ. Weakness worsens with activity and improves with rest. Ptosis and diplopia come first, then dysphagia and respiratory weakness.
  • Pyridostigmine: take on a strict schedule, about 30–60 min before meals to help chewing and swallowing. Never skip or double doses. Report SLUDGE signs, which point to cholinergic excess.
  • Crisis: both kinds threaten the airway. Monitor vital capacity and NIF and be ready to ventilate. Myasthenic crisis = too little drug; cholinergic crisis = too much, and atropine is the antidote.

3. Multiple Sclerosis (MS)

Study-guide highlights: Clinical manifestations; Uhthoff phenomenon

  • Autoimmune CNS demyelination. Relapsing-remitting is the most common form. Optic neuritis, diplopia, paresthesias, fatigue, spasticity, bladder dysfunction, Lhermitte sign.
  • Uhthoff phenomenon: heat (hot showers, fever, exercise) briefly worsens symptoms. Teach the patient to avoid overheating and use cooling strategies; it is not a new relapse.
  • Nursing: energy conservation, fall prevention, bladder program, skin care, and infection prevention, since infections trigger relapses.

4. Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS)

Study-guide highlights: Clinical manifestations; Priority assessment; Communication aids (AAC)

  • Upper and lower motor neurons degenerate: weakness, fasciculations, spasticity, dysarthria and dysphagia. Cognition and sensation usually stay intact.
  • Priority assessment: airway and breathing (FVC, cough strength, secretions), then aspiration and nutrition. Discuss ventilation and advance directives early.
  • Communication: set up AAC early (letter boards, eye-gaze devices, voice banking) while the patient can still learn it. Riluzole slows progression.

5. Meningitis

Study-guide highlights: Clinical s/s; Droplet precautions; Lumbar puncture nursing considerations

  • Classic triad: fever, severe headache, nuchal rigidity, plus photophobia and altered LOC. Kernig and Brudzinski signs. A petechial rash suggests meningococcal disease.
  • Bacterial meningitis needs droplet precautions (private room, surgical mask) until 24 h of effective antibiotics. Draw cultures and start antibiotics quickly; treat it as an emergency.
  • LP: patient voids first, then fetal or seated lean-forward position. Afterward, lie flat as ordered, push fluids, and watch for post-dural headache and site leakage. Bacterial CSF = cloudy, high protein, low glucose.

6. Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS)

Study-guide highlights: Clinical s/s

  • Autoimmune peripheral demyelination, often 1–3 weeks after a GI or respiratory infection (Campylobacter).
  • Ascending symmetric weakness with areflexia; can reach the diaphragm. Autonomic instability (BP and HR swings) can occur.
  • Priority: serial vital capacity and NIF, and prepare for intubation if they fall. Treatment is IVIG or plasmapheresis. Prevent DVT and manage pain.

7. Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA)

Study-guide highlights: Clinical s/s; DMARDs; Labs

  • Systemic autoimmune disease: symmetric small-joint synovitis, morning stiffness over 1 h, fatigue, low-grade fever. Ulnar drift, swan-neck deformity, rheumatoid nodules.
  • Labs: positive RF and anti-CCP, raised ESR/CRP, anemia of chronic disease.
  • DMARDs: methotrexate is the anchor. Give it weekly (not daily), with folic acid; monitor CBC, LFTs and renal function; no alcohol; contraception required. Biologics add TB and infection risk.

8. Osteoarthritis (OA)

Study-guide highlights: Clinical s/s; Nursing care plan

  • Degenerative 'wear and tear' cartilage loss in asymmetric weight-bearing joints. Pain worsens with activity and improves with rest. Morning stiffness lasts under 30 min. Heberden and Bouchard nodes.
  • Care plan: pain management (acetaminophen first, NSAIDs, heat or cold), weight loss, low-impact exercise, and assistive devices.
  • Nursing diagnoses: chronic pain, impaired physical mobility, fall risk. Teach joint protection and pre-op/post-op care for joint replacement.

9. Osteoporosis

Study-guide highlights: Prevention; Diagnostic testing (DEXA)

  • Prevention: calcium about 1,200 mg/day and vitamin D for older women, weight-bearing and resistance exercise, stop smoking, limit alcohol and caffeine, home fall-proofing.
  • DEXA T-score: −1 to −2.5 = osteopenia; −2.5 or lower = osteoporosis. Screen all women 65 and older (earlier if at risk).
  • Bisphosphonates (alendronate): take on an empty stomach with a full glass of water and stay upright 30 min to prevent esophagitis.

10. Gout

Study-guide highlights: Clinical s/s; Diet

  • Uric acid crystals deposit in a joint, often the great toe (podagra). The joint becomes suddenly red, hot, swollen and exquisitely painful, often at night. Tophi form in chronic gout.
  • Diet: limit purines (organ meats, red meat, shellfish, anchovies), alcohol (especially beer) and high-fructose drinks. Drink 2–3 L of fluid a day to prevent uric acid stones.
  • Drugs: colchicine and NSAIDs treat an acute flare. Allopurinol is for prevention, not acute attacks; teach fluids and to report any rash (SJS).

11. Ménière's Disease

Study-guide highlights: Clinical s/s; Hydrochlorothiazide

  • Excess endolymph in the inner ear causes episodic vertigo, fluctuating sensorineural hearing loss, tinnitus and aural fullness.
  • Attack care: lie still in a dark, quiet room, move slowly, fall precautions; meclizine or antiemetics as ordered.
  • Hydrochlorothiazide reduces endolymph. Pair it with a low-sodium diet (under 2 g/day) and limited caffeine and alcohol. Monitor for hypokalemia and orthostatic hypotension.

12. Breast & Gynecologic Cancers

Study-guide highlights: Breast cancer risk reduction; Sentinel lymph node biopsy care; Cervical cancer screening recommendations; Endometrial biopsy indications; Chemotherapy and fevers (neutropenic fever)

  • Breast cancer risk reduction: monthly breast self-awareness, mammography, limit alcohol, stay active, keep a healthy weight, breastfeed; BRCA counseling for high-risk patients. After SLNB/ALND: no BP, IVs or sticks on the affected arm, elevate it, and watch for lymphedema.
  • Cervical screening: Pap every 3 years from age 21–29. From 30–65, co-test every 5 years, HPV test alone every 5 years, or Pap alone every 3 years. Endometrial biopsy is indicated for postmenopausal bleeding or abnormal uterine bleeding at 45 or older.
  • Neutropenic fever (ANC under 500 plus temp at or above 38 °C / 100.4 °F) is an emergency: cultures, then broad-spectrum antibiotics within 1 h. Protective precautions; no rectal temps or suppositories.

13. Prostate Cancer

Study-guide highlights: Risk factors; Metastasis

  • Risk factors: age over 50, African American ancestry, family history and BRCA mutations, high-fat diet. Screening is PSA plus DRE.
  • Early disease is often silent. Later signs: urinary obstruction, hematuria, erectile dysfunction.
  • It spreads most often to bone (pelvis, spine), causing back pain, pathologic fractures and hypercalcemia. Watch for spinal cord compression, an emergency.

14. HIV & Opportunistic Infections

Study-guide highlights: Opportunistic infections; PEP and PrEP; Serodiscordant couple education

  • Opportunistic infections track the CD4 count. Below 200 (the AIDS threshold): PJP and toxoplasmosis; below 50: MAC and CMV retinitis. Others include candidiasis, Kaposi sarcoma and cryptococcal meningitis.
  • PEP: start as soon as possible, within 72 h of exposure, and take for 28 days. PrEP (daily oral pill or injectable) is for HIV-negative people at risk and needs HIV testing every 3 months.
  • Serodiscordant couples: U=U, since a sustained undetectable viral load means no sexual transmission. The negative partner may use PrEP, use condoms for other STIs, and test regularly.
